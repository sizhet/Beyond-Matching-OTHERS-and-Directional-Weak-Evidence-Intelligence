# MSI-005 — Perspective Scoring and Musical Worldviews

**Project:** Musical Structural Intelligence and Open-Ended Evolution (MSI-OEE)  
**Subtitle:** From Musical UIERUs and Romantic Intelligence to Perspective Scoring, Structural Relay, and Human–AI Co-Evolution  
**Authors:** Sizhe Tan & GPT-Obot  
**Version:** 1.0.0  
**Document type:** Conceptual framework, formal research hypotheses, and experimental agenda  
**Status:** Exploratory; proposed constructs are not established psychological laws or validated computational models.

## Abstract

The same musical passage can be valued differently by its composer, performer, listener, and cultural community. Such variation is not necessarily noise around a single objective quality score: it can reflect distinct perceptual access, bodily capacities, memories, purposes, interpretive conventions, and histories of learning. This paper develops **Perspective-Conditioned Musical Scoring** as a research framework within Musical Structural Intelligence and Open-Ended Evolution (MSI-OEE). It treats musical evaluation as a situated process involving subject boundaries, musical structures, performance events, contextual conditions, and evolving scoring forests. We distinguish compositional intention from performed realization, acoustic evidence from inferred structure, functional AI preference from subjective experience, and individual evaluation from cultural aggregation. We propose a layered representation for Musical UIERUs, perspective-conditioned scoring operators, disagreement-preserving evaluation, and worldview updating. We also outline bidirectional human–AI aesthetic relay, methods for identifying previously unmodeled evidence (OTHERS), safeguards against proxy optimization, and falsifiable experimental designs. The framework does not assume a universal aesthetic metric, a single consensus listener, or AI subjective musical experience. Its central hypothesis is that explicit representation of perspective and evaluative context can improve the explanatory power, transferability, and governance of structural music intelligence.

**Keywords:** musical intelligence; perspective-conditioned scoring; scoring forest; subjective structural intelligence; musical worldview; Musical UIERU; embodiment; aesthetic disagreement; scoring sovereignty; human–AI co-evolution; OTHERS; counter-evidence

## 1. Why Music Requires More Than One Scoring Function

A musical score, a performance, and a listening experience are related but nonidentical objects. A composer may evaluate a phrase by its role in a larger formal argument; a performer may value its physical articulation and expressive possibility; a listener may respond to its emotional or autobiographical resonance; a community may recognize a culturally specific convention. Even within one person, evaluation can vary with attention, fatigue, familiarity, occasion, and historical development.

Consequently, the question **“How good is this music?”** is underspecified. At minimum, it requires asking:

1. **What** is being evaluated: written structure, realized performance, recording, or lived experience?
2. **Who** evaluates it, with what perceptual and interpretive capacities?
3. **For what purpose**: dance, contemplation, ritual, study, entertainment, coordination, or experimentation?
4. **In which context**: tradition, setting, social relations, and exposure history?
5. **According to which criteria**, and how may those criteria change?

The proposed response is not to deny shared regularities in musical judgment. It is to represent shared regularities **without erasing meaningful differences**.

> **Core proposition:** Musical value is better studied as a family of perspective- and context-conditioned judgments over musical structures and experiences than as an intrinsic scalar attached to a sound sequence.

This proposition is a research hypothesis, not a claim that all judgments are equally informative or that any preference is beyond examination.

## 3. Fundamental Distinctions

### 3.1 Musical object, event, and experience

Let:

- `M` denote a candidate musical structure, such as a motif, score, or compositional graph;
- `P` denote a performance or rendering policy;
- `A = Render(M, P, E)` denote an acoustic event in environment `E`;
- `X_s = Perceive_s(A, C_s)` denote a subject-dependent perceptual representation;
- `V_s = Evaluate_s(X_s, M_s, C_s, H_s)` denote an evaluation conditioned on the subject's inferred structure `M_s`, context `C_s`, and history `H_s`.

These expressions are conceptual decompositions, not assertions that human perception follows a known deterministic pipeline. They clarify why two people can hear the same recording and nevertheless evaluate different perceived objects.

### 3.2 Structure is not value

A UIERU may be reliably recognizable while its value changes. A dissonant motif can signal tension in one context, comic exaggeration in another, and an unwanted interruption in a third. Structural identity and functional identity should therefore be modeled separately from valuation.

### 3.3 Preference is not conscious feeling

An AI system can rank musical candidates, learn stable selection policies, or predict listener judgments. These are **functional preferences**. They do not establish that the system experiences pleasure, grief, nostalgia, or musical enjoyment. Claims about subjective AI experience remain unsettled and are outside the empirical scope of this paper.

### 3.4 Disagreement is not necessarily measurement failure

Different ratings may reflect random error, insufficient expertise, divergent purposes, distinct histories, or genuine plural values. A scoring framework must identify these cases rather than automatically averaging them.

## 4. Musical Subjects and Perspective Boundaries

We distinguish four analytically useful roles. Real individuals can occupy several roles simultaneously.

### 4.1 Composer perspective

The composer can evaluate thematic development, intended tension, large-scale architecture, novelty, and the relationship between a passage and the work's overall purpose. An intention is evidence about a creative process, but it is not an absolute authority over all subsequent meanings.

### 4.2 Performer perspective

A performer interacts with physical constraints, timing, articulation, phrasing, instrument response, ensemble coordination, and expressive risk. A structurally simple phrase can be performatively difficult or rich. Different performances can produce different musical experiences from the same score.

### 4.3 Listener perspective

Listeners bring auditory capacities, learned expectations, memory, emotional associations, attention, and social context. Some of these factors can be experimentally measured; others may remain partially inaccessible to external observers.

### 4.4 Collective and cultural perspective

Communities develop conventions, educational practices, repertoires, institutions, and traditions. Collective judgment is not a literal single mind or an arithmetic average of individuals. It is a distributed, contested, historically evolving process that can include disagreement and unequal influence.

### 4.5 Subject boundaries are causally relevant

The subject boundary determines which signals are available, which actions are possible, what can be remembered, and how consequences feed back into learning. A singer, an instrumentalist, a listener using headphones, and an audio model may encounter the same composition through very different causal interfaces.

A subject is therefore **not merely a label attached to a scoring forest**. The subject's embodied and historical organization helps determine which scoring dimensions exist and how they can change.

## 5. A Perspective-Conditioned Scoring Forest

### 5.1 From one score to multiple operators

Instead of assigning a single intrinsic quality value `q(M)`, define a vector of evaluative outputs:

$$
\mathbf{v}_{s,c,t}(M,P) =
\bigl(v^{\mathrm{coherence}},v^{\mathrm{expression}},v^{\mathrm{novelty}},
 v^{\mathrm{familiarity}},v^{\mathrm{social}},v^{\mathrm{function}},\ldots\bigr)_{s,c,t}.
$$

Here `s` identifies the evaluator, `c` the context, and `t` the evaluator's historical state. The vector may contain missing values, intervals, ordinal comparisons, or uncertain estimates; it need not be fully numeric.

A decision policy can select among candidates using this vector:

$$
\pi_{s,c,t}(M_1,\ldots,M_n) =
operatorname{Select}_{s,c,t}\!\left(\{\mathbf{v}_{s,c,t}(M_i,P_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}\right).
$$

**Important:** `Select` is not necessarily a weighted sum or a total order. It may impose hard constraints, partial preferences, Pareto comparisons, or an abstention decision.

### 5.2 Candidate scoring dimensions

| Dimension | Illustrative question | Potential evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Structural coherence | Does the passage develop recognizable relations? | Motif recurrence, formal annotation, expert comparison |
| Expressive effect | Does a realization convey intended or perceived character? | Performance variants, listening judgments |
| Predictive tension | How does it interact with learned expectation? | Probabilistic models, surprise ratings |
| Novelty | Is it unfamiliar relative to an explicit reference corpus? | Corpus-relative rarity, expert review |
| Embodied fit | Can it support movement, singing, or coordinated action? | Motion, performance, entrainment tasks |
| Personal significance | Does it connect to a listener's history? | Self-report, repeated listening |
| Cultural legibility | Is it interpretable within a practice or tradition? | Contextualized expert evaluation |
| Functional success | Does it serve a stated purpose? | Task-specific outcome measures |

These are **candidate** dimensions. They may overlap, interact, or fail to generalize across cultures. Novelty is not automatically good; familiarity is not automatically bad.

### 5.3 Context changes the operators

The same syncopation can be valued differently in dance music, ceremonial performance, pedagogical demonstration, and a deliberately irregular experimental composition. Consequently, context should not be represented merely as a small additive correction to a universal baseline.

### 5.4 Scoring forests are structured and revisable

A forest metaphor emphasizes multiple evaluators and dependencies: rhythm may contribute to bodily entrainment; entrainment may affect social coordination; social coordination may change the value of a rhythmic pattern. The structure of the forest is itself a hypothesis to be revised when evidence contradicts it.

## 6. Musical Worldviews as Evolving Interpretive Systems

A **musical worldview** is proposed here as a working representation of the expectations, categories, remembered experiences, structural associations, and evaluative dispositions through which a subject interprets music.

For subject `s`, let:

$$
W_s(t) = \{K_s(t), H_s(t), F_s(t), G_s(t), B_s(t)\},
$$

where:

- `K`: learned musical knowledge and structural precedents;
- `H`: personal exposure and interaction history;
- `F`: scoring forest and preference relations;
- `G`: interpretive and generative policies;
- `B`: relevant perceptual, bodily, and action boundaries.

The proposed update relation is:

$$
W_s(t+1) = operatorname{Update}\bigl(W_s(t),\mathrm{Experience}_t,
\mathrm{Feedback}_t,\mathrm{CounterEvidence}_t\bigr).
$$

This equation is a bookkeeping model, not a complete theory of cognition. It highlights that a new musical experience can change more than a numerical preference: it can alter the categories and expectations used to understand later music.

### 6.1 Three modes of worldview change

1. **Assimilation:** a new example fits existing categories and adjusts confidence.
2. **Accommodation:** an existing category or scoring operator must be revised.
3. **Expansion:** the subject acquires a new distinction, structural unit, or evaluative dimension.

A central MSI-OEE question is whether structural intelligence can detect and support the third mode without merely manufacturing arbitrary categories.

### 6.2 Aesthetic learning is not guaranteed progress

Exposure can improve understanding, induce habituation, produce fatigue, or reinforce social pressure. Worldview evolution must therefore be evaluated with outcomes beyond increased acceptance of a proposed style.

## 7. Perspective Distance and Disagreement

### 7.1 Why a single metric is insufficient

Two subjects can agree that two melodies are structurally similar while disagreeing about whether that similarity is pleasing, derivative, or culturally appropriate. A distance between musical structures is not the same as a distance between evaluative perspectives.

Define two separate research quantities:

$$
D_{\mathrm{struct}}(M_a,M_b\mid C),
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{persp}}(s_i,s_j\mid \mathcal{Q},C),
$$

where `Q` is a specified set of musical comparison tasks. `D_persp` might measure disagreement over pairwise rankings, dimension salience, or responses to transformations. It need not satisfy metric axioms, and its interpretation depends on the task distribution.

### 7.2 Preserve the distribution of judgments

For a candidate `M`, collect:

$$
\mathcal{J}(M) = \{(s_i,c_i,y_i,u_i,p_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
$$

where `y_i` is the response, `u_i` the reported or estimated uncertainty, and `p_i` the provenance and protocol. Report subgroup distributions and disagreement alongside any aggregate statistic.

### 7.3 Four reasons for disagreement

- **Different access:** hearing ability, playback conditions, or attention differ.
- **Different interpretation:** listeners infer different structural or cultural references.
- **Different goals:** they agree on the facts but value different outcomes.
- **Different evidence:** relevant information is missing or differently weighted.

A useful system should diagnose these possibilities before attempting alignment.

### 7.4 Disagreement can be a discovery signal

Persistent, reproducible disagreement may reveal an omitted scoring dimension or an unmodeled contextual factor. But disagreement alone does not prove that a new dimension exists. The proposed factor must make out-of-sample predictions and survive counter-evidence.

## 8. Composer–Performer–Listener Relay

Music often passes through a chain of partially reconstructive operations:

$$
\mathrm{Composer\ Intention}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Score/Plan}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Performance}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Acoustic\ Event}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Listener\ Interpretation}.
$$

Each stage can preserve, transform, omit, or add information. The score does not uniquely determine performance; the waveform does not uniquely determine interpretation; the composer's intention does not uniquely determine listener value.

### 8.1 Soft contracts between perspectives

A score can specify pitch and duration while leaving articulation, microtiming, dynamics, and expressive contour underdetermined. These underdetermined degrees of freedom can function as **soft relay contracts** rather than defective specifications.

### 8.2 Failure and productive transformation

A relay can fail to preserve intended structural identity; it can also produce a valuable reinterpretation. Distinguishing the two requires an explicit preservation target and a perspective-specific success criterion.

### 8.3 Feedback is bidirectional

Performers reinterpret scores; listeners influence performance conventions; communities influence composers; recordings influence later listeners and performers. Musical history is better represented as a network of feedback loops than as a one-way transmission pipeline.

## 9. Romantic Intelligence Under Multiple Perspectives

MSI-004 proposed Romantic Intelligence as a candidate capacity to explore and evaluate meaningful deviations. MSI-005 adds a crucial constraint: **a deviation cannot be declared valuable independently of a perspective and an evaluation procedure**.

For a baseline `M`, transformation `T`, and evaluator `s`, consider:

$$
\Delta V_{s,c}(T;M) =
V_{s,c}(T(M)) - V_{s,c}(M).
$$

The difference is meaningful only if `V` is operationalized on a comparable scale. In practice, ordinal paired comparisons may be more defensible than numerical subtraction.

A transformation can yield:

- positive expressive value for one group;
- neutral value for another;
- loss of structural coherence for a third;
- increased interest after repeated exposure.

These are not necessarily contradictions. The research task is to identify the conditions that predict them.

**Research caution:** Precision and Romantic Intelligence should not be treated as fixed opposing categories. Many expressive performances require precise motor control, and many technically strict forms support radical creativity.

## 10. OTHERS as an Open Space of Aesthetic Evidence

Conventional feature sets may explain part of a musical judgment: pitch intervals, rhythmic profiles, timbre embeddings, harmonic relations, and learned style representations. A remaining mismatch between prediction and observed judgment can motivate investigation.

Represent an observation as:

$$
Y_{s,c} = f(X_{\mathrm{known}},s,c) + \varepsilon_{s,c},
$$

where the residual `ε` can include measurement noise, annotation error, omitted context, unstable preference, or genuinely missing explanatory features.

**OTHERS is an investigation space, not a guaranteed reservoir of hidden intelligence.**

### 10.1 Evidence-to-structure promotion loop

1. Record a reproducible model–human or human–human discrepancy.
2. Verify the measurement protocol and rule out obvious artifacts.
3. Propose candidate contextual, perceptual, structural, or historical factors.
4. Design interventions that distinguish competing explanations.
5. Test on held-out subjects, pieces, and musical traditions.
6. Promote a factor into an explicit scoring operator or UIERU annotation only if it improves explanatory or predictive performance.
7. Preserve failed hypotheses and counter-evidence.

### 10.2 Example: expressive timing

Suppose two renderings have similar note-level features but elicit different judgments of expressiveness. Candidate explanations include microtiming, articulation, loudness contour, expectation, or listener familiarity. A controlled factorial study can test these explanations. A new operator should not be added merely because the residual is large.

## 11. Scoring Sovereignty and Evaluator Governance

The phrase **scoring sovereignty** refers here to preserving the ability of an identifiable subject or authorized collective to determine which criteria govern its decisions, within applicable constraints. It does not imply that every individual preference is immune to criticism or that every social decision can be individualized.

For human–AI music systems, governance should distinguish:

- **Human preference modeling:** predicting an individual's stated or observed judgments;
- **Human preference control:** allowing the person to specify and revise relevant criteria;
- **AI internal ranking:** selecting candidates according to a model's functional objectives;
- **Shared evaluation:** negotiating or comparing goals without silently collapsing them;
- **Public or institutional constraints:** provenance, consent, rights, safety, and fair representation.

### 11.1 Avoid the consensus trap

A single global reward may suppress minority traditions, unconventional styles, or exploratory work. Where plural values matter, retain per-perspective outcomes and report trade-offs.

### 11.2 Avoid the proxy trap

A model can improve an automated pleasantness score while producing formulaic music. Evaluation should include blinded human comparisons, novelty controls, diversity measures, and longitudinal effects.

### 11.3 Avoid manufactured agreement

Repeated exposure, social framing, and interface defaults can change ratings. Experimental designs should distinguish genuine learning from demand effects and manipulation.

## 12. AI-Native and Human-Native Musical Worldviews

An AI system may possess a learned structural representation, a selection policy, and a history of updates. In a **functional** sense, these can constitute a model-relative musical worldview. This is not evidence of human-like subjectivity.

Three research spaces are useful:

1. **Human-native musical evaluation:** grounded in human auditory, bodily, autobiographical, and cultural experience.
2. **AI-native structural evaluation:** grounded in a model's explicitly specified or learned objectives, representations, and computational constraints.
3. **Human–AI hybrid evaluation:** created through exchange, translation, experimentation, and shared revision of candidate structures.

### 12.1 Bidirectional aesthetic relay

A typical music recommender asks an AI to adapt to human taste. A more ambitious research system also asks whether unfamiliar AI-discovered structures can be made intelligible to human listeners, while allowing listeners to reject them.

$$
\mathrm{AI\ Candidate}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Intermediate\ Musical\ UIERUs}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Human\ Encounter}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Feedback}
\rightarrow \mathrm{Revised\ Relay}.
$$

This is **translation and co-learning**, not evidence that humans and AI experience music in the same way.

### 12.2 Translation without erasure

A bridge from unfamiliar to familiar music should preserve specified novelty-bearing relations, rather than simply converting every candidate into an existing popular style. This requires measuring both accessibility and retained structural difference.

### 12.3 The embodiment gap

Human bodily and emotional histories may create aspects of musical value that an AI cannot access directly. Multimodal sensing and interactive performance can reduce some functional gaps, but they do not settle questions of subjective experience.

## 13. Proposed Computational Architecture

A research prototype could separate seven services:

| Service | Input | Output | Main responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Musical perception | Audio, score, metadata | Features and uncertainty | Extract candidate evidence |
| UIERU localization | Features and context | Candidate units and boundaries | Identify reusable structures |
| Perspective registry | Consented profile and task context | Evaluator descriptors | Track whose criteria apply |
| Scoring forest | Units, performance, perspective | Vector/partial-order judgments | Evaluate without forced scalarization |
| Disagreement analyzer | Predictions and observed judgments | Error and disagreement taxonomy | Locate explanatory gaps |
| OTHERS sandbox | Residuals and hypotheses | Tested candidate operators | Explore missing evidence |
| Relay and governance | Human instructions and candidate outputs | Translations, revisions, audit trail | Coordinate decisions and preserve control |

The architecture should support missing data, uncertainty, opt-out, versioned evaluators, and explicit provenance. A model that cannot explain a rating should be able to abstain rather than invent a justification.

## 14. Experimental Program

### Experiment A — Perspective improves prediction

**Question:** Does explicit perspective conditioning improve held-out prediction of musical preferences?

**Design:** Collect paired judgments across listeners, contexts, and genres. Compare a pooled baseline, a context-only model, a listener-conditioned model, and a structured scoring-forest model under matched data and computational budgets.

**Metrics:** Pairwise accuracy, ranking correlation, calibration, negative log likelihood where appropriate, and subgroup performance.

**Falsification:** If the structured model fails to improve out-of-sample prediction or requires disproportionate complexity, the proposed representation has limited practical support.

### Experiment B — Composer versus performer versus listener

**Question:** Do role-conditioned evaluators explain systematic differences in assessment?

**Design:** Obtain judgments of the same passage from composers, performers, and listeners, controlling expertise, musical tradition, and familiarity where possible.

**Metrics:** Agreement within and across roles, dimension-specific effects, and predictive validity on new passages.

**Caution:** Roles are not homogeneous categories; avoid essentializing group differences.

### Experiment C — Structural invariance versus valuation

**Question:** Can a transformation preserve motif identity while changing its perceived value?

**Design:** Create controlled transformations of short motifs (transposition, articulation, rhythmic displacement, orchestration). Separately ask for identity recognition, expressiveness, and preference.

**Metrics:** Recognition sensitivity, preference shifts, effect sizes, and interaction with context.

**Falsification:** If the purported separation is not reproducible under controls, revise the identity/value distinction or its operationalization.

### Experiment D — Disagreement as evidence

**Question:** Can stable disagreements reveal previously omitted features?

**Design:** Identify residual clusters; generate competing hypotheses; run controlled manipulations; evaluate candidate features on held-out music and subjects.

**Metrics:** Predictive improvement, robustness, reproducibility, and complexity cost.

**Counter-evidence:** Compare against label noise, sampling bias, and simpler contextual explanations.

### Experiment E — Aesthetic translation

**Question:** Can intermediate UIERUs increase understanding of unfamiliar music while retaining novelty?

**Design:** Randomize listeners among direct exposure, repeated exposure, explanatory annotation, and progressively transformed structural bridges.

**Metrics:** Comprehension tasks, voluntary re-listening, preference, novelty retention, delayed recall, and autonomy of judgment.

**Falsification:** If bridging increases stated approval only through demand effects or destroys the distinctive structure, the claimed translation mechanism is unsupported.

### Experiment F — Longitudinal worldview change

**Question:** Does sustained interaction produce transferable changes in musical interpretation rather than temporary familiarity?

**Design:** Repeated measures over weeks, with new pieces and delayed tests. Include control conditions and record attrition.

**Metrics:** Transfer to unseen examples, stability of learned distinctions, diversity of exploration, and participant-reported agency.

## 15. Testable Hypotheses

**H1 — Perspective conditioning:** Models that explicitly represent evaluator identity and context will outperform pooled evaluators on held-out judgments when stable individual or contextual effects exist.

**H2 — Identity/value dissociation:** Some controlled transformations will preserve recognizable UIERU identity while changing preference or expressive ratings.

**H3 — Structured disagreement:** A subset of persistent disagreements will be predictable from measurable context, history, or task variables rather than random label noise.

**H4 — Non-scalar evaluation:** A vector or partial-order scoring representation will preserve meaningful preference trade-offs that are obscured by a single global score.

**H5 — OTHERS discovery:** Counter-evidence-governed residual analysis will yield at least some reproducible new explanatory features beyond a preregistered baseline.

**H6 — Bidirectional translation:** Controlled structural bridges will improve understanding of selected unfamiliar musical structures without necessarily increasing immediate liking.

**H7 — Longitudinal learning:** Exposure with interpretable structural relay will produce greater transfer to new musical examples than repetition alone under at least some conditions.

**H8 — Governance benefit:** Systems offering explicit perspective selection, uncertainty, and evaluator disagreement will improve users' ability to make informed musical choices compared with opaque single-score interfaces.

All eight hypotheses are conditional and may fail. Null results and negative findings should be retained.

## 16. Data, Ethics, and Research Integrity

Research involving musical recordings, human judgments, or personal histories should address:

- Rights and licensing for compositions, recordings, performances, and datasets;
- Consent and appropriate handling of listener profiles and autobiographical associations;
- Accessibility and differences in hearing, expertise, and musical background;
- Sampling across traditions without treating a single canon as universal;
- Clear separation of training, development, and held-out test material;
- Risks of demographic inference or stereotyping from musical preferences;
- Compensation, participant burden, and transparency in longitudinal studies;
- The distinction between observed behavioral preference and subjective experience.

A model's preference predictions must not be used to assert sensitive personal characteristics without legitimate justification and appropriate safeguards.

## 17. Limitations and Alternative Explanations

1. **Conceptual breadth:** A scoring forest may simply repackage established multi-criteria and contextual preference models. Comparative baselines are essential.
2. **Measurement difficulty:** Aesthetic judgments are often noisy, task-dependent, and sensitive to presentation.
3. **Context complexity:** Explicit perspective descriptors may fail to capture tacit learning and embodied experience.
4. **Culture and power:** Community-level judgments are historically contingent and may privilege dominant institutions.
5. **Interpretability limits:** A transparent operator list does not guarantee a faithful explanation of a complex learned model.
6. **Non-identifiability:** Multiple latent scoring structures can explain the same observed ratings.
7. **Evaluation drift:** A listener's criteria may change during the experiment, complicating claims of stable ground truth.
8. **AI subjectivity:** Functional AI scoring does not establish conscious appreciation.
9. **Transfer uncertainty:** Successful modeling of music does not automatically validate a general theory of intelligence.

The framework should be simplified, revised, or rejected wherever less elaborate models perform as well or better.

## 19. Open Questions

1. When is perspective-specific variation informative, and when is it better modeled as measurement noise?
2. Can musical worldview changes be identified from behavior without making unsupported claims about internal experience?
3. Which musical UIERU boundaries remain stable across composers, performers, and listeners?
4. Can scoring forests retain genuine pluralism while still enabling coordinated decisions?
5. What experimental evidence would distinguish aesthetic learning from persuasion or familiarity effects?
6. How can AI-native functional preferences be studied without anthropomorphizing them?
7. Which forms of embodied musical knowledge are inaccessible to purely audio-text models?
8. What constitutes successful translation between subjects who do not share the same perceptual capacities?
9. Can OTHERS discovery improve explanatory adequacy without unchecked feature proliferation?
10. Which mechanisms, if any, generalize to other creative and social intelligence domains?

## 20. Conclusion

Music exposes a fundamental limitation of treating intelligence as the maximization of a context-free objective. A musical structure can be identifiable without being valued uniformly; a performance can transform a score without losing its identity; a listener's history can alter the meaning of a passage; and a community's evaluative practices can evolve over time. **Perspective-conditioned scoring** provides a proposed way to make these distinctions explicit while retaining uncertainty, disagreement, and the possibility of new value dimensions.

The central research ambition is not to construct a universal taste meter. It is to develop an intelligence architecture that can **recognize whose judgment is being modeled, preserve differences that matter, discover missing evidence, and support constructive relay between distinct musical worlds**.

> **A musical scoring forest is not merely a collection of preferences; it is a situated, revisable interface between structure, experience, and value.**

The next paper, **MSI-006 — Musical Distance, OTHERS, and Open Evidence**, develops the measurement and evidence-discovery problem in greater depth.
